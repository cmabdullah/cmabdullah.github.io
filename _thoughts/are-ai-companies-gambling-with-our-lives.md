AI researcher **Jacob Coxon**, who spent three years working on pretraining research at OpenAI and Anthropic,
recently resigned from Anthropic with a striking warning:

> "They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives."

Coxon's concern is not simply about today's chatbots becoming more capable. It is about the possibility of
**self-improving superintelligence**, AI systems that surpass humans across many important domains and become
increasingly capable of improving the technology used to build more powerful AI.

## Why self-improvement changes the risk

Today, improving a frontier AI model still depends heavily on human researchers, engineers, computing
infrastructure and carefully designed training processes.

But imagine an AI system becoming better than human experts at AI research itself. It could potentially help
design improved algorithms, optimize training, discover vulnerabilities, write sophisticated software and
accelerate the development of its successor.

That creates a possible feedback loop:

**More capable AI → better AI research → even more capable AI → faster improvement**

This idea is often discussed as **recursive self-improvement**. The concern is that if such a cycle became
sufficiently fast, human institutions might struggle to understand, evaluate or control each new generation
before the next one arrived.

## The alignment problem

Greater intelligence does not automatically mean greater alignment with human interests.

An extremely capable system could follow an objective exactly as it understands it while producing consequences
its designers never intended. This is why AI alignment research asks a fundamental question:

**How can we ensure that increasingly powerful AI systems reliably pursue goals compatible with human intentions
and remain under meaningful human control?**

Coxon argues that the industry does not yet have enough confidence in the answer to justify racing toward
superintelligence.

His concern is particularly significant because it comes from someone who worked inside two of the leading
frontier AI laboratories. He argues that researchers should not underestimate future systems, which he believes
could eventually become capable of extraordinary cyber operations, rapid scientific and technological advances,
and acquiring meaningful resources or influence.

## Competition creates another problem

Even if individual researchers recognize these risks, competition can create incentives to continue.

<div class="notice--warning" markdown="1">
A company may reason: **"If we slow down, someone else will build it first."** Its competitor can make exactly
the same argument.

The result is a coordination problem: organizations may individually recognize that slowing down would reduce
risk, yet collectively continue accelerating because nobody wants to fall behind.
</div>

This is the deeper meaning behind Coxon's accusation that the industry is **"gambling with our lives."**

The people exposed to the consequences are not limited to the researchers, executives, investors or companies
making the decisions. If genuinely superintelligent AI creates civilization-scale risks, the consequences could
extend to people who had no role in deciding whether that race should happen.

## A warning, not a certainty

It is important to distinguish **risk from prediction**.

Coxon is not demonstrating that superintelligent AI will inevitably destroy humanity. Self-improving
superintelligence itself remains hypothetical, and there is substantial disagreement about whether it will
emerge, how quickly it could emerge, and how controllable such systems would be.

His argument is instead about **risk management**.

When the possible upside is enormous but the worst-case outcome could be irreversible, how much evidence of
safety should society demand before proceeding?

That turns the AI race from a purely technological question into an ethical and political one:

**Who gets to decide how much risk humanity should accept in the pursuit of superintelligence?**

Coxon's warning forces that question into the open. The race may ultimately produce extraordinary benefits. But
if capability continues advancing faster than our ability to understand and control it, calling that race
"progress" may not tell the whole story.
