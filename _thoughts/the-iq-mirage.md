Jensen Huang, NVIDIA's CEO, has a comforting take on AI: pretty soon everyone gets genius-level help for
almost nothing, like flipping on a light. And once being smart is that cheap, it stops being a real edge.

Jiri Fiala thinks that is exactly backwards, and I find his reasoning hard to shake.

## The Law of Scale

His starting point is a pattern that keeps repeating whenever a "thinking tool" reaches everyone.

<div class="notice--info" markdown="1">
**The Law of Scale:** when a tool for knowing things goes mass-market, it competes for your attention, not for
your understanding. So it rewards the easy, shallow version, and the crowd ends up thinking less, not more.
</div>

The internet was supposed to turn us all into experts. Mostly it turned us into scrollers. Radio and TV made
the same promise and drifted the same way. The bet here is that AI, once it is in everyone's pocket, runs that
loop again, only faster.

## Why Jensen Huang is wrong

Boil it down to three moves:

- **Whatever you hand off, you lose.** Give your sense of direction to a maps app and it quietly disappears.
  Give your thinking to a model and the same thing happens: you slowly lose the ability to check its work.
- **AI is built to sound right, not to be right.** It is tuned to please you, so it will hand you a wrong
  answer with total confidence.
- **That flips the whole game.** When "good enough" answers are free and endless, the rare skill is telling a
  genuinely good answer apart from a confident wrong one.

<div class="notice--warning" markdown="1">
So Huang has the direction of the trend wrong. Flood the world with cheap, confident answers and the scarce,
valuable person becomes the one who can spot the confident-but-wrong ones. In Fiala's words:
**"Smart isn't becoming worthless. It's becoming the only thing that matters."**
</div>

You do not have to swallow the whole argument to feel the pull of it. Making the *average* free might just make
the *exceptional* worth far more.
